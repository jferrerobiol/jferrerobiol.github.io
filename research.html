Research

My research uses population genomics and phylogenomics to reconstruct the evolutionary history of birds, with a particular focus on seabirds. I am particularly interested in local adaptation and in the genetic basis of complex traits that allow species to thrive in their environments.

Past, present and future responses to climate change

Genomes record the evolutionary history of populations: growth and decline, when lineages split, whether they met again and which variants they exchanged. I use this record to provide background on how species responded to past climate, to explain the diversity we see today and, combined with climate projections, to forecast which populations will be able to adapt. During my PhD I dated the shearwater radiation and showed that its rapid diversification coincided with the palaeoceanographic changes of the late Pliocene. We showed that Europe's most threatened seabird, the Balearic shearwater, and its sister species, the Yelkouan shearwater, have repeatedly diverged during glacial periods and hybridised during interglacials. This gene flow has kept inbreeding and genetic load low and can buffer the negative effects of future bottlenecks. In European storm-petrels, demographic and divergence histories both track sea level: low sea levels during glacial maxima drove population declines and the divergence of lineages, and high sea levels drove population growth and expansion. In the lesser kestrel, a migratory raptor, we identified two evolutionarily distinct lineages with different past, present and future responses to climate change, which shows that species-level forecasts can mask divergent fates within a species. With the Bird Genoscape Project, I study local adaptation across 44 North American bird species sampled across their ranges, and use it to investigate repeated patterns of adaptation and to map their vulnerability to climate change.

The role of structural variation in adaptation

Structural variants such as inversions hold co-adapted alleles together, letting a complex phenotype be inherited as if it were a single gene. In the meadowlark species complex I work on the characterisation of a supergene controlling migratory behaviour (see The genetics of migratory behaviour). In the lesser kestrel, I am working on a large chromosomal inversion associated with female fertility. With Josephine Paris and Bonnie Fraser, we showed that the colour polymorphism of male guppies is governed by a large autosomal haplotype, and we tested how repeatable the signatures of balancing selection are across large and small populations. In brown trout, with Josephine Paris and Daniel Osmond, we used replicated metal pollution contrasts to study the repeated adaptation to metal pollution and identified a structural variant as the main candidate. To enable the study of structural variation, I have led or contributed to chromosome-level reference genomes for several bird species, to one of the first avian pangenomes, and to local and global genome sequencing efforts such as the Catalan Biogenome Project, the Bird 10,000 Genomes Project and the Vertebrate Genomes Project.

The genetics of migratory behaviour

Every year billions of birds migrate, and in many species a young bird makes its first journey alone, without ever having been shown the way. Experiments in the 1990s showed that several migratory traits are heritable, but the genes underlying them remain largely unknown. I collaborate with movement ecologists to combine tracking with genomic data and investigate the genetic basis of migratory traits. In the meadowlark species complex, I work on the characterisation of a supergene that controls migratory strategy across species and migratory direction in the Western meadowlark. In the contact zone between Cory's and Scopoli's shearwaters, we combined tracking and genomics and showed that birds follow the migratory strategy of their genomic ancestry rather than that of their colony, and that hybrids take routes intermediate between those of the two parental species. In black-legged kittiwakes, I work on the population of Middleton Island, Alaska, where some individuals migrate and others do not, and I use whole-genome sequencing of tracked birds to identify the genetic basis of partial migration.

Seabird phylogenomics & taxonomy

Shearwaters are a group of pelagic seabirds that diversified recently and rapidly, so their relationships were unresolved and their taxonomy is still debated. During my PhD I resolved the shearwater radiation by combining sequence capture of ultraconserved elements with ddRAD-seq, and showed that incomplete lineage sorting and introgression leave different footprints in each marker type, which explains why earlier studies disagreed. With Andreanna Welch's group, we then produced the first genomic phylogeny of the Procellariiformes and identified the predictors of substitution rate variation across the order. I also used range-wide genomic data to resolve the taxonomy of North Atlantic and Mediterranean shearwater populations at different stages of the speciation continuum. In this work I argue that conservation should protect intraspecific diversity and the processes that generate it, not species limits alone.
