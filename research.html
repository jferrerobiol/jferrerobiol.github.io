What I work on

Research

I combine whole-genome sequencing with tracking, field sampling and long-term monitoring to trace variation from the genome to the phenotypes it produces. Most of my work is on birds, and much of it on seabirds.

The genetic basis of migratory behaviour

Which genes, and which parts of the brain, decide where a bird goes

Migratory propensity and direction are heritable, but the genetic architecture behind them has been hard to pin down. I work on systems where migration segregates with identifiable genomic variation: a large chromosomal inversion that controls migratory direction and propensity across the meadowlark species complex, a contact zone where hybrid shearwaters take intermediate routes, a partially migratory kittiwake population, and a migratory raptor whose two lineages respond differently to climate change. The next step is to move from genotype to mechanism, asking in which brain cell types these variants act.

Seabird evolution: radiations, species limits and hybridisation

How pelagic seabirds diversified, and what keeps them apart

Shearwaters and petrels radiated rapidly and recently, which makes their history hard to read and their taxonomy contested. Using sequence capture, RADseq and whole genomes, I have resolved the shearwater radiation, dated it against late-Pliocene oceanographic change, and used genomic data to settle species limits in a speciation continuum of threatened taxa. More recently this work has turned to hybridisation: recurrent gene flow from a congener is what prevents inbreeding in Europe's most threatened seabird, and hybrids in the Cory's–Scopoli's shearwater contact zone show that migratory strategy follows genomic ancestry rather than colony.

Local adaptation and conservation genomics

Genome–environment associations across whole ranges, and what they mean for management

With the Bird Genoscape Project I study repeated local adaptation across dozens of North American bird species using low-coverage whole-genome sequencing sampled across each species' range, and use it to map vulnerability to climate change. The same tools apply to small and isolated populations: the Eurasian woodcock's Macaronesian populations, the Balearic shearwater, Europe's most endangered reptile and its hybridisation with an invasive congener, and brown trout persisting in metal-polluted rivers. I try to produce results that can be used directly in management decisions.

Reference genomes and structural variation

The resources that make the rest possible, and the large variants they reveal

I have led or contributed to chromosome-level reference genomes for the black-legged kittiwake, Cory's shearwater, the Eurasian woodcock, the Balearic shearwater, the king penguin, the barn swallow and the European lobster, and I am part of the Vertebrate Genomes Project and the Bird 10,000 Genomes consortium. Good assemblies expose the large structural variants that short-read studies miss, and increasingly these turn out to matter: a supergene controlling migration in meadowlarks, an inversion associated with female fertility in a colonial raptor, an autosomal haplotype governing colour polymorphism in guppies.
